These are gross areas. The measured ones live under “2D plans”. Everything in the table above is taken to the structural grid, which is the right basis for comparing floors and for a first pass at cost. It is not what you can lay a tile on. The room schedule on the Schedules & checks tab of the 2D plans is generated from the model itself and gives clear areas, with each bounding wall’s thickness deducted — so the living room is 32.8 m² there against the 35.0 m² its 5.0 × 7.0 m grid would suggest, and the master 28.5 m² against 30.7. Those figures cannot drift, because they are computed from the same coordinates that build the 3D. The ones in this document are typed, and can.
What the bigger upper floor costs, stated plainly. Enlarging the first floor to 188.3 m² buys the master suite, the library, the family room and the 27.2 m² balcony, and it is the right move for how the house will actually be lived in. But it is not free on paper: because the upper floor now oversails the ground floor front and back, it — not the ground floor — is what a planning authority measures. Building coverage rises from 26.9 % to 36.3 %, and total hard surface from about 47 % to about 58 %.

36.3 % is still inside the residential coverage limit normally applied in Lagos State, but it is no longer comfortably inside it, and the margin you were carrying has gone. Confirm the coverage limit for your street with the Physical Planning Permit Authority before drawings go in. If it comes back tighter than 36 %, the cheapest fix is to pull the rear cantilever back from 2.40 m to about 1.20 m — that costs roughly 16 m² off the master bedroom and bedroom 2 and brings coverage back to about 33 %.

The rear of the plot: the garden

The sports court has been removed. The rear of the plot is a garden and nothing else, which means the Garden / Sports court switch is gone from the top bar along with the court's slab, netting, floodlights, benches and equipment locker — and the games tent is now simply there, rather than being something the court could take away. The one thing kept from that block is the PV array, which never belonged to the court in the first place and sits flat on the main roof deck behind the parapet.

What this frees up. Roughly 130 m² of concrete raft and acrylic surfacing, and with it ₦8.5–15 m of build cost and the annual upkeep of an acrylic surface. The ground goes to lawn, planting and the kitchen garden. If you ever want the court back it is the same rectangle, so nothing here forecloses it.

Getting around

Walk mode used to grab the pointer as soon as you clicked: the cursor vanished, the mouse drove the view continuously and Escape was the only way out. That is how a first-person game works and it is not how anyone expects to look round a building. It now works the way Street View does — press and drag anywhere to look, double-click the floor to walk there (double-tap on a phone). WASD and the on-screen pad still work for fine positioning, and the old game-style mouse-look is still available on Shift+click for anyone who wants it.

The double-click target is raycast into the scene and then tested against the same collision model the walking uses: the surface has to be near-horizontal, the destination has to be clear of every collider, and it has to be on the storey you are already standing on. If the exact point is occupied the search spirals outward to the nearest legal standing spot, which is what stops a click on the arm of a sofa doing nothing at all. The camera then eases to the target rather than snapping, so you keep your bearings on arrival.

It does not check that a walkable route exists. If you can see a patch of lawn through a window you can click it and you will be standing on it, without going round by the door — same as Street View. That is the right trade here; the alternative is pathfinding, which is a lot of machinery to stop somebody looking at their own garden.

The carport

The canopy is now a cantilever off the boundary wall rather than a table on five posts. Three of those five stood along the house side, directly in the path a car takes swinging out of its bay towards the gate; taking them out is what makes the drive-out manoeuvre honest rather than decorative.

Be clear about what that asks for structurally. The deck now runs 6.50 m off the wall with nothing underneath its free end. A flat concrete slab will not do that. It wants tapered steel cantilever beams at about 2.75 m centres — deep at the root, thin at the tip — tailed back into a reinforced pier built into the boundary wall, with the wall and its foundation designed for the overturning moment. That is a real engineering item and a real cost line, not a detail. Price it with a structural engineer before it goes on a drawing; the taper you can see in the model is standing in for it, not specifying it.

Cooling

The ceiling fans have gone. Every habitable room has a wall-mounted split AC instead — a 1.0 m indoor unit at 2.55 m with a return grille and a tilted discharge louvre. Four rooms previously carried a fan and a split unit fighting each other across a 3.0 m ceiling; the living room had only the fan and now has a unit of its own.

The honest trade. A fan costs almost nothing to run and keeps working when the mains do not. Splits do neither — on this compound they run off the standby generator, which is why the genset enclosure matters more than it looks. Budget roughly ₦450–900k per room installed for a 1.5 hp inverter split, plus the load on the generator: eight units at about 1.1 kW each is nearly 9 kW before anything else in the house is switched on.
The utility yard. The generator house, the changeover panel and the drying line sit hard against the rear wall in a 1.4 m service strip, and the soakaway covers are on the east side. The genset enclosure is the small pitched-roof structure standing behind the games tent — it now reads as one, with a full-height louvred intake, a discharge louvre on the return, an exhaust stack up past the roof line and an access door on the west end.

What removing the BQ costs you. This is worth stating plainly, because it is a real trade and not a free win. You lose two self-contained lettable units (roughly ₦18–24 m of build cost, but also whatever they would have earned or housed), and you lose on-plot accommodation for domestic staff or extended family. If either of those matters, the honest alternatives are a first floor over the carport at the front, or a single stacked unit in the north-east corner — both of which can be built into this model.

What you gain is the whole rear of the plot: ≈ 162 m² in one piece, 19.5 m wide and 8.3 m deep, against the 4.9 m band the BQ used to leave. Building coverage falls from 36.2 % to 26.9 %, and total hard surface from 55.8 % to about 47 %.
On the sports brief. Worth recording why nothing was built for it. Site strategy

  • Setbacks: 10.4 m front to the main wall (8.2 m to the balcony edge), 3.0 m both sides, 3.02 m rear. Confirm the exact requirement for your street with the Lagos State Physical Planning Permit Authority before drawings go in.
  • Building coverage 36.3 % — up from 26.9 % now that the enlarged first floor, not the ground floor, sets the footprint. Still inside the residential limit normally applied, but confirm it. Total hard surface including paving is about 58 %.
  • Raised plinth of 600 mm on the duplex. This is the Badagry axis: sandy soil, a high water table and seasonal surface water. Do not build at grade.
  • Orientation: modelled with the street to the south, so the long elevations face east and west. The 2.10 m cantilevered roof wing over the balcony does the shading work at the front; the parapet roofline elsewhere carries no eave overhang, which is the flat-roof trade-off — window reveals and sills do more of the work on the side elevations than they would under a deep eave. If your frontage actually faces another direction, tell me and I will re-orient the plan — it changes where the master and the living room want to sit.
  • Cross-ventilation in every habitable room — each has openings on two sides or a window opposite its door.
  • Parking for three cars under an 8.25 m × 6.50 m carport canopy, on a 7.32 m front yard. The depth is set by the longest vehicle: a full-size pickup is 5.89 m, which leaves 0.55 m behind it and 0.51 m between its nose and the gate. Use the Open gate button and the three vehicle buttons in the top bar to drive them in and out - the route is the real one, and it is the reason the house sits where it does.
  • Drainage: septic tank and soakaway in the rear utility yard, at the maximum practical distance from the borehole, with a perimeter drain running to a rear soakaway pit.

What this model is and isn’t

This is a design study, dimensionally consistent and buildable in principle: every wall, opening, stair riser and setback in the walkthrough is at true scale, and you can measure off it. It is not a construction drawing set. To build, this needs to go to a registered architect for the Lagos State planning submission, plus a structural engineer for the raft and frame design and an MEP engineer for services. Take these files to them — it will save you a great deal of the briefing.

Nothing outside your boundary is drawn. Earlier versions massed the neighbouring compounds so the plot did not float in space. They have been removed: every building you can see in this model stands on your 655 m². The street, kerb and boundary wall remain, and the land beyond the wall is left as open ground — that is a drawing convention, not a survey of what is actually next door.

Downloaded assets, and the credit they are owed. Almost everything here is still generated in the browser at load, but five things are not, and they are the reason the page is now about 13 MB rather than 2 MB. On a slow connection the building appears first and these arrive after it — nothing waits on them.

  • Sky — Kloofendal 48d Partly Cloudy (Pure Sky) HDRI, Poly Haven, CC0. This is what every reflection in the glass, the cars and the polished floors is actually reflecting.
  • Coconut palm — low-poly model, 1,412 triangles, cloned fourteen times.
  • Broadleaf trees — two species from one file, cloned for the fruit trees. These replaced the alpha-card canopies, which read well at distance but had no branch structure behind the leaves.
  • Bush — 320 triangles, cloned about ninety times for the shrubs and the tops of the hedges. The hedge bodies stay as clipped boxes; only the top edge, where the eye reads the soft silhouette, is real foliage.
  • Vehicles — a Ford F-150, a generic low-poly SUV (mk2design, CC BY) and a Hyundai i30 N, each scaled so its length matches the real vehicle's published length.
A correction worth recording. Two of the three vehicle models shipped flat fans of geometry projecting from the headlights and tail lights to fake a beam. Authored for an unlit renderer, they came through this scene's materials as solid black wedges hanging off the front of each car — the black projections you could see from the lights. They have been stripped.

The visible artefact was the smaller half of the problem. Those beams ran past the bodywork: the truck's measured 6.20 m nose to tail against a body of 4.80 m. Since each model is scaled by matching its longest axis to the real vehicle's published length, the scaling had been sizing the beam to 5.89 m and leaving the actual F-150 at about 4.50 m — a quarter under size. The hatchback was 21 % under for the same reason. Both are correct now, which means the driveway clearances quoted on this page are only true as of this revision: at its real width of 2.48 m across the mirrors the truck takes noticeably more of the apron than the undersized one did, and the three bays have been re-spaced to give four equal 400 mm gaps rather than the old even 2.67 m centres.

The vehicle and plant models are third-party work under Creative Commons terms. If this drawing is ever put to any use beyond your own, check each model's licence on its source page first — some are CC BY (credit required, commercial use fine) and some are CC BY-NC (no commercial use at all), and the difference matters.

Rendering. Every other surface is physically based and procedural: colour, tangent-space normal, per-texel roughness and ambient occlusion, all generated in the browser at load. Lighting is a sun on a continuous day cycle plus image-based lighting from the captured sky above. Tone mapping is ACES Filmic through a post chain of screen-space ambient occlusion, bloom, colour grading and SMAA, with a little depth of field in the dollhouse view only. Glazing is real refracting glass at IOR 1.5.

The sun slider in the top bar runs from 05:36 to 19:24 and drives sun angle, colour, sky, cloud cover, exposure and the reflection probe together. It defaults to 17:18, which puts the sun at about 21°. That is not an arbitrary choice: an overhead sun is the worst light a building can be shown in, because nothing casts a shadow across a facade and every surface flattens out. The fluted panels, the sills and the plinth band are all drawn to be read in raking light.

Performance. Four quality presets, from Low to Ultra, switchable at any time and picked automatically on first load from what the device looks like; a watchdog measures the real frame rate and steps down a level at a time until it holds, and stops the moment you choose a preset yourself. Underneath, the static geometry is merged by material at load, which took the model from about 2,600 objects and 2,000 draw calls to under 400 and 330 — roughly a fourfold speed-up on integrated graphics, and the reason the higher presets are usable at all. Press G to force the refracting glass on or off, whichever way you prefer the trade.

Planting is cut-out foliage cards, not solid blobs: each canopy is a handful of intersecting quads carrying a leaf-clump texture, with the vertex normals pushed outwards so they shade like a mass of leaves rather than like flat panes, and a slow wind in the vertex shader. Leaf size is set per plant type so a hedge leaf and a mango leaf are the right size relative to each other.
