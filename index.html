Two different numbers, and the second is the one you can see. Building coverage — the 36.2 % a planning authority asks for — counts only what sits under a roof. Walking the model you also see the driveway, walkways, terrace and service pads, and those read as built even though no permit counts them. The first version of this design paved about 211 m², which put the total hard surface at roughly 68 % and is exactly why the open space was not visible. This revision cuts paving to about 128 m²: the west side path is now stepping stones in grass, the east path is 1.1 m instead of 1.8 m, the rear terrace is pulled tight against the house, the BQ apron is a 1.1 m doorstep strip and the utility yard is two working pads rather than a full-width slab. That is ≈ 83 m² of concrete returned to lawn.

What will not fit, at any price. Tennis needs 36 × 18 m of run-off-inclusive space; the whole plot is 33.5 m long, so it does not fit even on bare land. Five-a-side needs 25 × 15 m and volleyball 24 × 15 m — both out. A full basketball court is 28 × 15 m. What does fit is one multi-use hard court, which is what is drawn: full-size badminton and pickleball, a residential basketball half court, and a goal for a kickabout. A club would want 1.50 m of side run-off; this has 0.95 m, and nothing that keeps the four-bedroom footprint would give more.

Cost, order of magnitude. Concrete raft and acrylic surfacing ≈ ₦5–8 m; hoop, goal, netting and poles ≈ ₦2–4 m; floodlighting ≈ ₦1.5–3 m. Against that, building the court means not building the BQ, which saves roughly ₦18–24 m — but it also loses two lettable units, the domestic staff accommodation and the indoor games room. Get a local contractor to price the court before deciding; these are planning figures, not a quotation.

The utility yard moved. The generator house, tank stand and drying line used to sit 3 m out from the rear wall, in the middle of the only ground wide enough for a court. They are now pulled hard against the wall in a 1.4 m service strip, and the soakaway covers have gone to the east side. That change applies in both modes.

Recreation & compound

You chose lawn and garden over marked courts. The 418 m² of open compound breaks down as:

  • Rear garden, ≈ 80 m² of lawn — the strip between the house and the BQ. It is 17.0 m wide but only 4.9 m deep, and the terrace takes a 6.0 m bite out of the middle of it, so the largest single clear rectangle is about 13.6 m × 2.5 m, with two 4.5 m × 4.9 m pockets either side of the terrace. Good for a hammock, a table, a paddling pool or a five-a-side kickabout across the width. Not a field.
  • Front garden, ≈ 30 m² of open lawn east of the entrance walk, with palms, a flower bed, a shade tree and a water feature pushed onto the boundary so the middle stays clear.
  • West flank, ≈ 40 m² — a 2.3 m grass strip with stepping stones instead of a paved path, running from the driveway to the gazebo.
  • Timber gazebo, 3.4 m × 3.4 m — pyramid roof, built-in seating, lamp overhead. Moved into the west corner so it no longer stands in the middle of the lawn.
  • Rear terrace, 6.0 m × 2.4 m — paved, tight against the kitchen and rear lobby, with a six-seat dining set, a parasol and a masonry barbecue.
  • Indoor games — the 21.3 m² games room in the BQ block, plus the 14.7 m² upstairs family lounge.
  • Utility yard behind the BQ — generator house, tanks on a stand, septic and soakaway covers, drying lines, its own service gate.
Straight answer on “a lot of space”. A 156 m² house plus an 81 m² BQ plus three parking spaces on a 655 m² plot does not leave a big garden, and cutting the paving back does not change that — it only makes the garden you do have visible. The plot is 19.55 m wide, the side setbacks take 6.0 m of it, and the front setback and the BQ take 13.0 m of the 33.52 m depth. What is left is a 4.9 m band. Three moves would actually change it, and any of them can be built into this model:
  • Slide everything 2 m forward — front setback 7.0 m down to 5.0 m (still legal on a local road with a sliding gate; confirm for your street). Rear garden goes from 4.9 m to 6.9 m deep, giving ≈ 110 m² in one piece. Costs nothing.
  • Stack the BQ — unit B over unit A instead of beside it. The block shrinks from 13.55 m to 6.8 m wide, opening a 6.8 × 6.0 m corner and taking the contiguous lawn to ≈ 120 m². Adds roughly ₦6–10 m for the extra slab and stair.
  • Move the BQ out of the back garden entirely — two units and the games room as a first floor over the carport at the front. The whole rear becomes one garden, ≈ 200 m², 17 m × 12 m. Adds roughly ₦18–25 m and puts service circulation at the front rather than the back, which some owners want and some do not.
The first two together give ≈ 150 m² for about ₦6–10 m. Tell me which and I will rebuild the model.
On the sports brief. A regulation tennis court needs 648 m² and a regulation basketball court 420 m² — both larger than the entire open compound. If you want marked play, the rear lawn as drawn will take a 13.6 m × 2.5 m practice strip only; a proper 13.6 × 6.0 m mini court (basketball half-court to a hoop on the BQ wall, plus badminton or pickleball) needs one of the three moves above first, and then costs roughly ₦4–7 m in surfacing, netting and line-marking.

Site strategy

  • Setbacks: 7.0 m front, 3.0 m both sides, 3.02 m rear. Confirm the exact requirement for your street with the Lagos State Physical Planning Permit Authority before drawings go in.
  • Building coverage 36.2 % — comfortably inside the residential limit. Total hard surface including paving is 55.8 %, down from 68 % in the first version.
  • Raised plinth of 600 mm on the duplex and 450 mm on the BQ. This is the Badagry axis: sandy soil, a high water table and seasonal surface water. Do not build at grade.
  • Orientation: modelled with the street to the south, so the long elevations face east and west. The 2.05 m front canopy and 680 mm eaves do the shading work. If your frontage actually faces another direction, tell me and I will re-orient the plan — it changes where the master and the living room want to sit.
  • Cross-ventilation in every habitable room — each has openings on two sides or a window opposite its door.
  • Parking for three cars under a 7.0 m × 4.8 m carport canopy, with the 4.5 m sliding gate lined up on it.
  • Drainage: septic tank and soakaway in the rear utility yard, at the maximum practical distance from the borehole, with a perimeter drain running to a rear soakaway pit.

What this model is and isn’t

This is a design study, dimensionally consistent and buildable in principle: every wall, opening, stair riser and setback in the walkthrough is at true scale, and you can measure off it. It is not a construction drawing set. To build, this needs to go to a registered architect for the Lagos State planning submission, plus a structural engineer for the raft and frame design and an MEP engineer for services. Take these files to them — it will save you a great deal of the briefing.

Nothing outside your boundary is drawn. Earlier versions massed the neighbouring compounds so the plot did not float in space. They have been removed: every building you can see in this model stands on your 655 m². The street, kerb and boundary wall remain, and the land beyond the wall is left as open ground — that is a drawing convention, not a survey of what is actually next door.

Rendering. Every surface is physically based: colour, tangent-space normal, per-texel roughness and ambient occlusion, all generated in the browser at load rather than downloaded, which is why the whole thing is still one file. Lighting is a sun with 4096 px soft shadows plus image-based lighting from the sky dome, tone-mapped ACES Filmic. Glazing is real refracting glass at IOR 1.5. That last one costs an extra full pass over the scene each frame; if the frame rate cannot carry it the model quietly drops the glass back to a reflective pane rather than becoming a slideshow. On integrated graphics at full-screen you should expect roughly 15–25 fps at full screen; on a discrete GPU it is smooth. Press G to force the refracting glass back on, or off, whichever way you prefer the trade.
